# 66 — The Drone (slug: drone)
Idea: one note held underneath everything while the melody moves above it.
Songs (part 1):
- Amazing Grace on the bagpipes (melody PUBLIC DOMAIN — "New Britain", 1829; you may play it, in A or G): the Highland bagpipe has drones (tuned to A) sounding under the chanter's melody the whole time.
- Indian classical music: the tanpura plays a constant drone, usually of the home note and the fifth, under every raga.
- Tomorrow Never Knows — The Beatles · 1966: the whole song sits on a single C drone (copyrighted, name only).
Why (part 2), all true:
- A drone fixes home permanently: you can never forget where the tonic is.
- Every melody note is then heard against it: the fifth sounds sweet, the octave pure, the second rubs, the tritone grinds. The drone turns each interval into a colour.
- It's one of the oldest forms of harmony: bagpipes, the hurdy-gurdy, and early medieval organum.
- When a held note sits in the bass of a piece with changing chords, it's called a pedal point — named after the organ's pedals.
Essence: one note that never moves — and every other note discovers its own colour against it.
