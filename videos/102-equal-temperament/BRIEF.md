# 102 — Equal Temperament: Every Piano Is Slightly Out of Tune (slug: equal-temperament)
Idea: to play in every key, modern tuning divides the octave into 12 identical half steps — and makes almost every interval slightly impure.
Songs (part 1):
- Bach, The Well-Tempered Clavier, Book 1 (1722), PUBLIC DOMAIN: preludes and fugues in all 24 major and minor keys, showing that a "well-tempered" keyboard could play in every key. You may play the first chords of the Prelude in C (C – Dm/C – G7/B – C).
- Barbershop quartets and a cappella choirs often tune their chords pure by ear, so their chords "ring" in a way a piano can't (general).
Why (part 2), all true:
- Pure intervals come from simple ratios: fifth 3:2, major third 5:4. But stacking pure fifths overshoots by the Pythagorean comma.
- Older tunings kept some intervals pure; then music in distant keys sounded harsh (a badly out-of-tune fifth was called the "wolf").
- Equal temperament spreads the error evenly: every half step is the same ratio (the 12th root of 2, about 1.0595).
- Result: fifths are a tiny bit narrow (barely audible), but major thirds are noticeably wider than pure — they shimmer, or "beat".
- Bach's tuning was a "well" temperament, not necessarily exactly equal; equal temperament became standard later.
Essence: a small compromise in every interval — and the freedom to play in every key.
