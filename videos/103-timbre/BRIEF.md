# 103 — Timbre: Why a Violin Doesn't Sound Like a Flute (slug: timbre)
Idea: the same note on two instruments: same pitch, same loudness, different sound. That difference is timbre (tone colour).
Songs (part 1):
- Peter and the Wolf — Sergei Prokofiev · 1936 (copyrighted, name only): every character is an instrument — the bird a flute, the duck an oboe, the cat a clarinet, the wolf French horns.
- Ravel's Boléro passes one melody from instrument to instrument (we saw it): the tune stays, the colour changes.
Why (part 2), all true:
- Almost every musical note is a stack of frequencies: the fundamental plus overtones at 2×, 3×, 4×... the frequency.
- The recipe — how loud each overtone is — gives each instrument its colour. A flute's tone is close to pure, with weak overtones; a violin's is rich in overtones; a clarinet in its low register emphasises the odd ones (3×, 5×, 7×).
- The attack and decay matter too: a piano note starts with a hammer strike and fades; a violin can swell.
- Show overtone bars growing/shrinking for each instrument (visual: a.grid or bars), while the same note plays.
Essence: pitch tells you which note — timbre tells you who's singing it.
