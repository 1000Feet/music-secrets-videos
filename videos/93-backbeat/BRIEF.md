# 93 — The Backbeat (slug: backbeat)
Idea: in rock and pop, the snare hits beats 2 and 4 — the "weak" beats.
Songs (part 1) — copyrighted; play generic grooves only:
- Tutti Frutti — Little Richard · 1955: New Orleans drummer Earl Palmer, who played on Little Richard's and Fats Domino's records, is credited with popularising the strong rock 'n' roll backbeat.
- We Will Rock You — Queen · 1977: stomp, stomp, CLAP — the clap is the backbeat (no drums at all).
Why (part 2), all true:
- In marches and polkas the accent falls on 1 (and 3). Moving the accent to 2 and 4 creates a push-pull: the kick grounds you on 1 and 3, the snare answers on 2 and 4.
- That contrast makes the body move: you step on 1, you clap on 2.
- Audiences used to march music clap on 1 and 3; rock, gospel and R&B audiences clap on 2 and 4.
- Backbeat comes from African-American music: gospel, rhythm and blues, then rock 'n' roll.
Essence: accent the weak beat — and the whole song starts to swing your body.
Visual idea: circle:false grids of 4 beats, kick on 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4, and a "clap on 1 and 3 vs 2 and 4" comparison.
