# 83 — The Stars and Stripes Forever (slug: stars-and-stripes)
Idea: how a march is built: steady steps, strains, and a trio where countermelodies pile up.
Songs (part 1) — PUBLIC DOMAIN, but do NOT reconstruct Sousa's melodies from memory; play an oom-pah march grid (2/4), chords in E-flat major, then the trio in A-flat major, and your own short high piccolo-like trill line:
- John Philip Sousa, The Stars and Stripes Forever (1896). Since 1987 it has been the official National March of the United States.
- Sousa was known as "The March King".
- The famous high piccolo countermelody appears in the final trio.
Why (part 2), all true:
- 2/4 time: ONE-two, ONE-two — left, right. Bass on the beat, chord off the beat: the oom-pah of a marching band.
- A march is built from strains (sections) that repeat, then a trio: a contrasting section in a new key, usually a fourth higher (the subdominant). Here E-flat major moves to A-flat major: it feels broader and warmer.
- In the last trio Sousa stacks layers: the melody, the piccolo's countermelody above, and a low brass countermelody below — three lines at once, all fitting together.
Essence: steady steps, a new key, and layers that stack — that's how a march lifts a crowd.
