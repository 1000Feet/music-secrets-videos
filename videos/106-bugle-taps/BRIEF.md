# 106 — Taps: Music from Physics (slug: bugle-taps)
Idea: a bugle has no valves or keys: it can only play notes of the harmonic series of its tube — and those notes form a major chord.
Songs (part 1) — PUBLIC DOMAIN, you may play it (written in C; play it in C, around G4–G5):
- Taps — the US military bugle call, arranged in 1862, during the American Civil War, by Union General Daniel Butterfield with bugler Oliver Wilcox Norton, adapted from an earlier call.
- Melody: G G C(long) | G C E(long) | G C E, G C E, G C E(long) | C E G(long), E C G(long) | G G C(long).
- Other bugle calls, like Reveille, use the same few notes (general).
Why (part 2), all true:
- Blow harder or softer into a tube and it jumps between its natural overtones: ratios 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 of the lowest frequency.
- In C those are C, G, C, E, G — a major chord! The major triad is built into the physics of vibrating air.
- That's why every bugle call sounds like a broken major chord — only C, E and G are available.
- The next overtone (7×) is a flat B-flat that doesn't match the piano — so bugle calls avoid it.
- Valves on a trumpet change the tube length, giving a new harmonic series for every valve combination.
Essence: one tube, a few overtones — and nature hands you a major chord.
