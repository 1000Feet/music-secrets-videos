# 132 — The Theremin: Music Without Touching (slug: theremin)
Idea: an electronic instrument played by moving your hands in the air.
Songs (part 1) — copyrighted film scores: play only your own sliding theremin-like phrases (engine tone with a pure sine and pitch bend):
- Invented by Leon Theremin (Lev Termen) around 1920 in Soviet Russia.
- Its eerie, gliding sound became a hallmark of 1950s science-fiction films, such as The Day the Earth Stood Still (1951), scored by Bernard Herrmann.
- Clara Rockmore became the instrument's most celebrated virtuoso (general).
Why (part 2), all true:
- Two antennas: moving the right hand toward the vertical antenna raises the pitch; the left hand near the loop antenna controls the volume.
- The hands disturb an electric field — the player never touches the instrument.
- No keys, no frets: the pitch is continuous, so every move becomes a slide (glissando), and shaking the hand creates vibrato.
- Its tone is close to a pure sine wave — few overtones, which makes it sound ghostly and voice-like.
- Hitting exact notes in the air is notoriously hard: the player must listen constantly.
Essence: no keys, no touch — just a hand in the air and a voice from another planet.
Visual idea: a smooth pitch curve over the keyboard/circle (walker), a volume bar for the other hand.
