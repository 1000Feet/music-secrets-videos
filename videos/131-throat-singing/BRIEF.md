# 131 — Throat Singing: Two Notes from One Voice (slug: throat-singing)
Idea: Tuvan and Mongolian throat singers hold a low note and make its overtones sing a separate melody.
Songs (part 1) — play only your own demonstrations: a low drone with individual harmonics (8th–12th) brought out in turn (use the engine's tone option to emphasise one harmonic at a time):
- Khoomei — the throat-singing tradition of Tuva (Russia) and Mongolia.
- Styles include sygyt (a high, whistling overtone melody) and kargyraa (a very deep, growling sound).
Why (part 2), all true:
- Every sung note is a stack of harmonics: the fundamental plus 2×, 3×, 4×... the frequency.
- By shaping the mouth, tongue and throat, the singer boosts one harmonic at a time so strongly that it is heard as a second, whistle-like note.
- The low note stays the same (a drone); the melody comes from moving between harmonics.
- So the melody can only use notes of the harmonic series — around harmonics 8 to 12 they're close enough together to make a scale.
- Our mouth acts like a filter: its resonances (formants) are what turn "ah" into "ee" — throat singers push this to the extreme.
Essence: one voice, two notes — and the harmonic series becomes a melody.
