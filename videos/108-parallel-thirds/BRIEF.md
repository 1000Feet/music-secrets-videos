# 108 — Singing in Thirds (slug: parallel-thirds)
Idea: the classic vocal harmony: a second voice singing the same melody a third above (or below).
Songs (part 1) — copyrighted: do not play the songs' melodies; demonstrate with Twinkle Twinkle or Frère Jacques (public domain) harmonised in thirds:
- The Everly Brothers (e.g. Bye Bye Love, 1957): Don and Phil's close harmony.
- Mariachi, Mexican trios and Neapolitan songs often harmonise their melodies in thirds (general).
Why (part 2), all true:
- Octaves and fifths blend so well they almost merge into one sound. Thirds blend sweetly but stay distinct: you hear two voices, one colour.
- To stay in the key, the harmony voice alternates between major thirds (4 half steps) and minor thirds (3 half steps), following the scale.
- Flip a third upside down and you get a sixth: parallel sixths sound just as sweet.
- Classical counterpoint rules allowed parallel thirds and sixths, but not parallel fifths or octaves, because those make the voices lose their independence.
Essence: two voices a third apart — close enough to blend, far enough to be two.
