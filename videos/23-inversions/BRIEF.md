# 23 — Chord Inversions (slug: inversions)
Idea: the same three notes, but a different note in the bass. C/E (first inversion), C/G (second inversion).
Examples (part 1):
- A Whiter Shade of Pale — Procol Harum · 1967 · copyrighted: the bass walks down the scale step by step while the chords change above it — play a generic stepwise descending bass under changing chords in C: C, Em/B, Am, Am/G, F, F/E, Dm, Dm/C (do NOT claim these are the exact song chords; say "a bass that walks down step by step").
- Air on the G String — J.S. Bach · public domain: the famous slow walking bass under the melody (the song above was inspired by Bach). You may play a descending bass line in octaves.
Why (part 2), all true:
- Root position (C in the bass) sounds solid and finished.
- First inversion (E in the bass) sounds lighter, like it is still moving.
- Second inversion (G in the bass) sounds unstable and wants to move — classical composers used it right before the final cadence.
- Inversions let the bass move by step instead of jumping — that's how smooth, singable bass lines are built.
Essence: same chord, new foundation — the bass note decides how a chord feels.
