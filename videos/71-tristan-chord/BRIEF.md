# 71 — The Tristan Chord (slug: tristan-chord)
Idea: one chord at the start of Wagner's Tristan und Isolde that refuses to resolve — and changed harmony forever.
Songs (part 1) — PUBLIC DOMAIN, you may play this exact opening:
- Richard Wagner, Tristan und Isolde (composed 1857–59, premiered in Munich, 1865). Opening: cellos play A3, leap up to F4, then fall E4, D#4. On D#4 the chord F3–B3–D#4–G#4 sounds: the Tristan chord. Then the oboe rises G#4, A4, A#4, B4 while the harmony moves to E7 (E3 G#3 D4, with B4 on top). Then silence.
- The phrase is repeated, each time a little higher, with silences in between.
Why (part 2), all true:
- Its lowest two notes, F and B, form a tritone, so the chord is full of tension; its notes sound like a half-diminished seventh (like F–Ab–Cb–Eb), but they behave differently, so the ear can't tell where it's going.
- It moves to E7, a dominant chord, which should resolve to A minor — and it never does. Silence instead.
- That's the point: an opera about longing, written with harmony that keeps longing. The full resolution is withheld for hours, until the very end of the opera.
- Musicians have debated how to analyse this chord for over 150 years; it's often seen as a gateway to modern harmony.
Essence: one chord that never comes home — and music learned to long for something.
