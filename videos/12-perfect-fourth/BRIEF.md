# 12 — The Perfect Fourth (slug: perfect-fourth)
Idea: 5 half steps (C up to F, or G up to C). Ratio 4:3. Very often the first leap of a famous melody: from the fifth note below up to home.
Songs (part 1) — all public domain, melodies can be played, in C:
- Here Comes the Bride (Bridal Chorus) — Richard Wagner · 1850: opening G3 C4 C4 C4 ("Here comes the bride").
- Amazing Grace — Traditional: G3 C4 (then E4 C4 E4 D4 C4 A3 G3).
- Auld Lang Syne — Traditional: G3 C4 (then C4 C4 E4 D4 C4 D4 E4).
Why (part 2), all true:
- The fourth is the fifth turned upside down: C up to G is a fifth, G up to C is a fourth. Together they make exactly one octave (4:3 × 3:2 = 2).
- Leaping from the fifth note below up to the root sounds like an announcement / an arrival: the melody "lands" on home on a strong beat (pickup note → downbeat).
- In harmony a fourth over the bass sounds unresolved and wants to fall to the third — that's the sus4 chord (Csus4 → C, F falls to E).
- Medieval monks sang in parallel fourths as well as fifths (organum).
Essence: fourth and fifth are the two halves of the octave — a door out (fifth) and a door back home (fourth).
