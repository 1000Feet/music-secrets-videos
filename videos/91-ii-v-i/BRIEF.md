# 91 — The ii–V–I: Jazz's Favourite Sentence (slug: ii-v-i)
Idea: Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7 in C: two falling fifths, D → G → C.
Songs (part 1) — copyrighted, chords only:
- Satin Doll — Duke Ellington & Billy Strayhorn · 1953 · in C: Dm7 G7 Dm7 G7 | Em7 A7 Em7 A7 | Am7 D7 Abm7 Db7 | Cmaj7 — chains of ii–V's, each one a step higher.
- Autumn Leaves and Fly Me to the Moon are built from ii–V–I's too (both appeared in our circle-of-fifths video).
Why (part 2), all true:
- The roots fall by fifths twice: D → G → C, the strongest move in harmony, done twice in a row.
- ii (Dm7) prepares the dominant: it shares most of its notes with IV (F A C), the "pre-dominant" function.
- Guide tones: the seventh of each chord falls a half step into the third of the next: C (7th of Dm7) → B (3rd of G7); F (7th of G7) → E (3rd of Cmaj7). Two quiet half steps carry the whole progression.
- Jazz players treat ii–V's as building blocks, moving them to new keys (Satin Doll's ii–V's climb a whole step) — and swapping in the tritone substitution (Abm7 Db7 instead of Dm7 G7).
Essence: two falling fifths and two tiny half steps — the sentence jazz says a thousand ways.
