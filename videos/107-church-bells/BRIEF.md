# 107 — Why Bells Sound Sad (slug: church-bells)
Idea: a bell's overtones are different from a string's: it contains a strong MINOR third.
Songs (part 1) — PUBLIC DOMAIN, you may play this:
- The Westminster Quarters (Big Ben's chime): composed in 1793 for Great St Mary's Church in Cambridge, later used at the Palace of Westminster (Big Ben, 1859). First quarter, in E major: G#4 F#4 E4 B3.
- English change ringing: bells rung in changing orders (permutations) rather than tunes (general).
- In the 17th century, the Dutch Hemony brothers became famous for tuning carillon bells accurately (general).
Why (part 2), all true:
- A string's overtones follow 1, 2, 3, 4, 5... (they include a major third). A bell's main partials are different: the hum (an octave below), the prime, the tierce (a MINOR third above), the quint (a fifth), and the nominal (an octave).
- So every bell note carries its own minor chord inside it. Even a major tune on bells has a faintly melancholy, minor colour.
- Bell founders tune these partials by shaving metal from inside the bell.
- The partials don't line up perfectly with the piano, which gives bells their shimmering, slightly strange sound.
Essence: every bell sings a minor chord inside itself — that's why bells sound bittersweet.
