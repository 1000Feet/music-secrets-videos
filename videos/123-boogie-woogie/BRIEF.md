# 123 — Boogie-Woogie (slug: boogie-woogie)
Idea: a rolling left-hand pattern that turned the blues into a dance — and fed straight into rock 'n' roll.
Songs (part 1) — copyrighted recordings: play only the generic pattern below over a 12-bar blues in C:
- "Pinetop's Boogie Woogie" by Pinetop Smith (1928) gave the style its name.
- The 1938 "From Spirituals to Swing" concert at Carnegie Hall, featuring pianists Albert Ammons, Meade Lux Lewis and Pete Johnson, helped launch a boogie-woogie craze.
Why (part 2), all true:
- The left hand plays a steady pattern of eighth notes up and down the chord plus the sixth and flat seventh. In C: C E G A Bb A G E — repeated, moved to F and G for the IV and V chords.
- It runs over the 12-bar blues (I – IV – V).
- The eighth notes are usually swung, giving a rolling, driving feel.
- The right hand plays bluesy riffs and repeated chords on top — two independent layers.
- Early rock 'n' roll guitar riffs (like Chuck Berry's) adapted these boogie bass patterns.
Essence: one rolling left hand, eight notes per bar — and the blues started to dance.
Visual idea: walker on the circle tracing C E G A Bb A G E; 12-bar grid.
