# 160 — Why the Star-Spangled Banner Is So Hard to Sing (slug: star-spangled-banner)
Idea: the US anthem uses a borrowed English club song with a huge vocal range.
Songs (part 1) — PUBLIC DOMAIN, you may play the opening (in C): G3 E3 C3 E3 G3 C4 — down the major chord and back up past the start ("O say can you see").
- Lyrics by Francis Scott Key (1814), written after watching the bombardment of Fort McHenry.
- The melody is "To Anacreon in Heaven" by John Stafford Smith, written for the Anacreontic Society, a London gentlemen's music club (often called a drinking song).
- It became the official US national anthem in 1931.
Why (part 2), all true:
- The melody spans an octave and a fifth (a twelfth) — wider than most untrained voices are comfortable with.
- It opens by outlining the major chord downward and back up (5 3 1 3 5 8), then climbs higher later.
- The highest note comes near the end, on "land of the free" — so if you start too high, you run out of voice.
- Singers often choose a lower key, or embellish with melismas (see our melisma video).
Essence: a twelve-note range and a high note at the end — the anthem is a vocal obstacle course.
Visual idea: a range bar over the keyboard; the opening arpeggio on the circle; the high note highlighted.
