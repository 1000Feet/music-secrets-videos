# 13 — The Major Third (slug: major-third)
Idea: 4 half steps (C to E). Ratio 5:4. It is the note that makes a chord "major" and bright.
Songs (part 1) — public domain, in C:
- When the Saints Go Marching In — Traditional: C4 E4 F4 G4 (opening rises a major third).
- Swing Low, Sweet Chariot — Traditional: "Swing low" falls a major third, E4 → C4.
- Beethoven's Fifth Symphony — 1808: G G G Eb, the famous motif falls a major third (G4 G4 G4 Eb4, played short-short-short-long).
Why (part 2), all true:
- 5:4 → E is the 5th harmonic of a low C (harmonics of C2: C2, C3, G3, C4, E4…).
- Root + major third + fifth = the major chord, which sits right inside the harmonic series (4:5:6).
- On a piano (equal temperament) the major third is about 14 cents wider than the pure 5:4 — the most out-of-tune interval on the keyboard; choirs and string players often narrow it by ear.
- Two major thirds stacked (C E G#) make the augmented chord; three of them come back to the octave (the circle split in three — a triangle).
Essence: one interval, four half steps, and the whole world of "major" — brightness is built into physics.
